# Worked Examples and Optional Depth

Use these cards after the corresponding core teaching. They supply the scenario and a sample response; no external research, new account, real-world contact, or private disclosure is required. The examples are fictional teaching cases, not real measurements, medical guidance, legal rulings, financial advice, or promises of outcomes.

## Two kinds of optional work

- **Supplied practice:** the scenario, sample response, and depth question are on this page. Allow about 15–20 minutes for one card, with paper or oral explanation. Read the card before the session; a calculator is optional for arithmetic.
- **Open research prompt:** a request to locate or construct missing teaching material, such as outside sources, interview records, or a real-case packet. This is not supplied instruction. A tool activity with complete steps is still supplied instruction, even when an adult must prepare the device or account. An adult must select and verify materials, arrange access, and plan additional time first. Use the supplied card instead when those materials are unavailable.

Choose depth by readiness and interest, not age alone. Learners aged 10–12 can use the core teaching without taking every technical add-on; older-learner work remains optional. The depth questions are not prerequisites for the next core week or criteria for its checkpoint.

## How to use a card

1. Read the scenario and ask for a reasoned answer.
2. Let the learner draw, speak, sort, or write using the fictional case.
3. Compare with the sample: notice the evidence and the limits, not matching wording.
4. If ready, change the stated assumption using the depth question.
5. If the core idea is unclear, return to the linked lesson before adding complexity. A private or paper artifact is enough.

Sample responses illustrate reasoning, not scripts to memorize or the only defensible answer to an open choice. They do not require a learner to experience or disclose the situation.

## Week 1
**Core lesson:** [Week 1: What IS Media?](./week01-week-1.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Compare two fictional notices: "Club at 3" in plain text, and the same words beside a flashing BUY NOW badge. What changed?

**Illustrative response:** Both are made media, but the badge changes layout and persuasion. Construction choices can guide attention even when the meeting text is unchanged.

**Optional depth:** Identify author, audience, and purpose without assuming that every constructed message is false.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 2
**Core lesson:** [Week 2: Who Made This and Why?](./week02-week-2.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional poster says "Visit our shop"; a timetable lists opening hours; a comic tells a joke. Name their likely purposes.

**Illustrative response:** They may persuade, inform, and entertain respectively. A message can combine purposes, so the label is a starting explanation, not proof of intent.

**Optional depth:** Give a poster that both informs and persuades and explain how you can see each purpose.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 3
**Core lesson:** [Week 3: The Invisible Choices](./week03-week-3.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Two charts show fictional visits of 40 and 60. One starts at 0, the other at 35. Test "fivefold growth."

**Illustrative response:** The true ratio is 60/40 = 1.5, with 20 more visits or 50% growth. The cropped bars have visible lengths 5 and 25; that visual ratio is not the data ratio.

**Optional depth:** Write a caption limited to the two measured sessions, with units and source note; do not infer cause.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 4
**Core lesson:** [Week 4: The Re-Edit](./week04-week-4.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Facts: a dog crossed a yard, then sat. Make two captions while preserving those facts.

**Illustrative response:** "The dog crossed the yard and settled down" feels calm. "The dog dashed across the yard before stopping" changes tone only if dashed is supported; otherwise keep crossed and change layout or music.

**Optional depth:** Circle every factual addition. Explain how a re-edit can shift feeling without inventing an event.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 5
**Core lesson:** [Week 5: The Price of Free](./week05-week-5.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional free video page shows an advertisement before the clip. What exchange might be happening?

**Illustrative response:** An advertiser may pay for audience attention. Free access for the viewer does not mean no one funds the service or that all free services use this model.

**Optional depth:** Name another possible funding source, such as donations, and explain what evidence would distinguish it.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 6

**Supplied scenario and question:** Rewrite "You WON’T BELIEVE this library disaster!" using supplied facts: one activity room closed for one day for repair.

**Illustrative response:** "Library activity room closes for one day for repair." It conveys the known event without inflating its scope or relying on an emotional gap.

**Optional depth:** Say what additional evidence would be needed to call the event a disaster; do not infer it from the headline.

## Week 8
**Core lesson:** [Week 8: Selling Ideas](./week08-week-8.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional message says "Everyone loves Plan A." A card says 8 of 10 voluntary respondents chose A. Evaluate it.

**Illustrative response:** The survey supports 8/10 respondents, not everyone. A small voluntary sample may not represent the wider group.

**Optional depth:** Rewrite the claim with its denominator and sample limit; separate emotional appeal from evidence.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 9

**Supplied scenario and question:** Three sites repeat a closure claim from one post; a supplied library notice says temporary repair and gives a reopening date. How many independent confirmations are the reposts?

**Illustrative response:** The three reposts trace to one origin. The notice offers relevant evidence about the planned closure; its scope and timing still need checking.

**Optional depth:** Draw the origin chain and explain why repetition is not independent confirmation.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 10
**Core lesson:** [Week 10: The Fact-Check Sprint](./week10-week-10.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** The complete fictional packet gives counts 40 and 60, plus a headline saying "doubled." Write a verdict.

**Illustrative response:** The headline is contradicted: doubling 40 would be 80. The two sessions show a 50% rise, and do not establish the whole library’s trend.

**Optional depth:** Include claim, originating evidence, arithmetic, verdict, and remaining limits. Speed is not the learning goal.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 11
**Core lesson:** [Week 11: Spotting Fakes](./week11-week-11.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A supplied photo caption names today, but its source card dates the same photo two years ago. What is wrong?

**Illustrative response:** The photo may be genuine while the current-event context is false. A mismatch in date is evidence about this claim, not proof that every pixel was fabricated.

**Optional depth:** State what is known about the image separately from what remains unknown about the event.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 12

## Week 13
**Core lesson:** [Week 13: The Echo Chamber](./week13-week-13.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** A fictional feed shows five repeats of one viewpoint from the same source. Does seeing it often prove it?

**Illustrative response:** No. The feed can amplify repetition. Check originating evidence and credible alternatives instead of treating familiarity or agreement as verification.

**Optional depth:** Distinguish encountering another perspective from giving every claim equal evidential weight.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 17
**Core lesson:** [Week 17: Testing and Refining](./week17-week-17.md)

**Supplied scenario and question:** Two pretend readers could not find the tray location in a draft. What should change?

**Illustrative response:** Add a clear location label or simple diagram, then test again. Feedback identifies a communication problem, not a reason to shame readers.

**Optional depth:** Check both accuracy and clarity; a persuasive draft can still omit an essential fact.

**Facilitator check:** Ask which stated fact or assumption supports the explanation. If that link is missing, model it with the sample and invite a second explanation. Assess reasoning and limits, not speed, exact wording, or personal experience.

## Week 18
**Core lesson:** [Week 18: The Signal Broadcast](./week18-week-18.md)
